> [!record-opening] VISTA SENSOR INTERCEPT // RESIDENCE MONITORING CH-4
> *ORIGIN: Perimeter Sensor Array Station 4 (Site-29 North)*
> *SOURCE: Passive acoustic pickup, communal mess & sniper rooftop observation post*
> *TIMESTAMP: 03 Sep 2008 05:04:12 UTC*
> *SUBJECT: Vanny (SIGINT specialist / Household youngest)*
> *OPERATIONAL CONTEXT: Routine early-morning household assembly prior to scheduled perimeter relay maintenance. Eleven verified occupants logged in communal space. Subject Northrad (Lykor) stationed continuously on exterior roof.*

## I

"So it's not that it's *bad*," Vanny said, "it's that it's a skill issue, and the skill issue is the firmware, and the firmware is from before I was born, basically, and I'm telling you, if Bizmo prints the housing, I can-"

"Eat," said Hanktonio.

"I'm eating. I can eat and explain. That's called multitasking."

"That's called talking with your mouth full."

"Same thing, different latency."

The kitchen was full. It was always full by five, though no one had called anyone. Gunter was at one end of the table and Grunt was at the other, and they were not speaking, which in that house was its own kind of noise. Salmon was on his third plate of eggs and telling Medic, for the fourth time, about the pirates. Medic was nodding in the way of a man who had heard it and had decided not to count. Wendy sat with his back to the wall, fiddling with an airsoft magazine, his eyes on nothing. Markless ate with his head down. Andrew had taken the chair by the door and did not seem sure he was allowed to.

Verdansk was somewhere near the middle. Vanny noticed him only when he reached for the salt and Verdansk was already holding it, and had apparently been holding it for a while.

"Oh," said Vanny. "Hey. You're here."

"I'm here," said Verdansk.

"Cool. Great. Thanks." He took the salt. He had the strange sense, as he often did, that he had just spoken to something that had been in the room the whole time and would be again.

Kaliber put a cup in front of him. Two fingers tapped on the chest, then a palm pushed flat toward the floor: *Sit. Slow down.* Vanny sat. He had been sitting. He slowed down for roughly four seconds.

"Okay, but the *cupboard,*" he said to Joey, who was at the stove. "I put the spare batteries in the left one. You moved them."

"I put them where they go."

"They go where I *put* them."

"They go in the cupboard marked batteries."

"That's not a rule. That's your handwriting."

Joey did not turn around. "Resources come through me."

"Logistics come through *me.*"

"That's the same thing."

"That's what I'm *saying.*"

Neither of them had ever worked out where one ended and the other began. They argued about it most mornings, and neither of them had ever wanted it settled.

Across the table, Leepy lifted his head slowly. He had been listening the whole time, with the flat, pleasant attention of a man at the bottom of a warm pool.

"Left cupboard," he said. "Batteries were in the left."

"*Thank* you."

"I moved them," said Leepy, "to the right."

"Leepy."

"It was a long night." He smiled, slow and entirely without guilt. Vanny felt the old warmth in his chest and could not stay angry. Nobody else in the house could do that. Leepy was the only person who had never once asked him to be quieter.

## II

He was the youngest. It was in the way people held their cups when he came in, and in the order they spoke when he did. When he walked the yard, Gunter's hand found the back of his jacket, steering him around nothing. The jacket was too big. It was always too big, a modified tactical thing over a vest, sleeves pushed to the elbow, and from a distance more than one visitor had mistaken him for a girl, or a boy, or something in between. Vanny had stopped correcting it. It was one more thing nobody quite knew.

He did not mind being the youngest. He minded being *handled.*

He wore the headphones around his neck at the table and over his ears everywhere else. The laptop went with him from room to room in its rubber casing, its corners scarred and its fan always running. He carried it the way other men carried their sidearms. He did not carry it at the table, because Hanktonio had asked him not to, once, gently, in the voice that made refusal feel like theft.

Gunter and Grunt had begun again.

"It's the grenades," Gunter said.

"You said that yesterday."

"It was true yesterday."

"Then who covers the lane?"

"Whoever's on the lane."

"That's *me,* that's what I keep-"

Kaliber set down the kettle and tapped the table twice. *Enough.* Vanny watched them subside. He had a half-formed bet with Bizmo, who never came to breakfast, that someday one of them would say the answer aloud and the house would fall down.

## III

Konish came in at the end, the way the room's temperature changes before you see the weather.

He did not sit. He stood at the head of the table and took a cup from Kaliber, and everything stopped. Vanny's mouth was open. He closed it.

"Vanny," said Konish.

"Yes."

"The relay in the north mast."

"Yeah, I was going to-"

"Today."

"I was going to do it today."

"Before noon."

Vanny nodded. He felt the weight of it settle, and with it something like relief. It was not that he disliked being told. It was that he disliked not knowing what he was supposed to do, and Konish always knew. Hanktonio, down the table, gave him the smallest nod. *Go.*

Konish drank. He looked at the far end of the table, at the empty chair, and then at the plate Joey had set on the counter under a cloth.

"Someone take that up," he said.

## IV

The plate was warm. That was the part Vanny kept thinking about afterward. It was warm in his hands, the one thing in the house that was warm and meant for someone alone.

He took it up. He was not asked. He found himself on the stairs with it, and nobody called after him, and he understood in the half-dark of the third floor that this was the kind of permission that came from nobody saying no.

The hatch was heavy. He put his shoulder to it and the cold came over his face.

Lykor was at the edge, the rifle case beside him. Beside the case, on a folded cloth, lay a rangefinder, a spotting scope, and a thing Vanny could not identify.

He saw the thing and forgot everything else.

"What's *that?*"

"Put the plate down."

"Is that a thermal clip-on? That's not issue. Is that-"

"The plate."

Vanny set it down. He did not move any closer to the thing, but he was aware of every inch between them, as one is aware of the edge of a table.

"I'm going to eat," Lykor said, "and you're going to stand there."

"I can do that."

"You can't. But try."

Lykor ate. Vanny stood. It lasted perhaps eleven seconds.

"Does it feed to anything? Like, is there a wireless link, or is it just glass, because if it's glass I'm gonna be so sad-"

"It's glass."

"Oh." Vanny exhaled. "That's actually kind of beautiful."

Lykor looked at him. It was the first time he had.

"Where's your coat?"

"This is my coat."

"That's a jacket."

"It's a *modified* jacket."

"It's cold."

"You're not wearing one either."

"I'm not twelve."

"I'm not *twelve.*" Vanny tugged at the sleeve, then stopped, because he had been about to say something he had not meant to say and it was already gone. He looked at the dark instead. "Okay. What's the thing. The thing you're doing."

"Which thing."

"Up here. Every night. You just look."

"I count."

"Count what?"

"The ways in. Do you know them?"

Vanny knew them. He had logged them in the first week, on the laptop, in a file named something that was a joke. "Road. East track. Creek bed."

"Three."

"That's all of them."

"That's three."

He waited. Lykor ate. Vanny looked at the tree line, at the place where the ground fell away, and felt the shape of something missing. He pulled up the map in his head, the sensor placements, the dead spots. There was a gap. He could almost see it. He could almost-

He did not say it. It was not that he was afraid of being wrong. It was that he suddenly, strongly, did not want to be the one who had said it first.

"Okay," he said.

"Okay," said Lykor.

## V

"Can I ask something?"

"You will."

"Why do you eat up here?"

"It's quiet."

"The kitchen's not that loud."

Lykor did not answer. Below them, through the roof, Vanny could hear the house the way he always could: Gunter's laugh, a chair scraping, Salmon's voice rising over the pirates, and under it, steady as the fan on a laptop, the sound of eleven men not being alone. He had never thought of it as loud. He had thought of it as the sound of being fine.

"I'd go insane up here," Vanny said.

"I know."

"Do you ever want to come down?"

"No."

"Not even-"

"No."

Vanny nodded. He was quiet for nearly a full minute, which for him was a long time. Then, because he was Vanny, he said, "Leepy says you're the only one who doesn't need to be fixed."

"Leepy talks too much."

"Leepy doesn't talk at *all.*"

"Then he said it very slowly."

It was almost a joke. Vanny laughed, and this time it was not wrong, and Lykor's mouth moved by a degree that could have been nothing.

When the plate was empty, Vanny took it. At the hatch he stopped, with one hand on the rung.

"What do I call you? I mean. I heard, the other week-"

"What do the others call me?"

"Northrad."

"Then that."

"And him? The other one?"

"He's the only one who gets to."

"Okay." Vanny waited. "Is it a secret? Like, a real one?"

"It's a name."

"That's not an answer."

"No," said Lykor. "It isn't."

## VI

*(Continued in The Thirteenth. The two scenes share the roof, and the same unfinished question about the fourth approach.)*

Hanktonio was at the foot of the stairs when he came down. Vanny nearly walked into him.

"Where were you?"

"Roof. Plate."

"You were asked to do the relay."

"I'm *doing* the relay. I'm doing it right now. Look, I'm walking toward it."

Hanktonio's face did something that had, in an earlier version of the man, been a flash of anger. It was gone almost before it began, and what remained was the patience that had kept the house together longer than anyone could say.

"Before noon."

"Before noon," said Vanny. "I know. I got it. Yes."

He went. He passed the second-floor hall, where eleven doors stood open and one stood wider than the rest, and Leepy's lamp was on, and from inside came the slow sound of a man tying his boots on the right feet.

"Hey," said Leepy.

"Hey."

"What's he like?"

Vanny stopped. He thought about the glass, and the cold, and the way the man had said *three* as though it were a gift and a test at the same time.

"Quiet," he said.

"Louder than us?"

"No," said Vanny. "The opposite."

He took the laptop from the shelf. He put the headphones on. On the roof above him, the footsteps began again, slow and even, counting the approaches.

He did not know the fourth. He thought, for the first time, that he might not want to.
