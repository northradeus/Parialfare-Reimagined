## I

He watched her for forty minutes before he said a word.

Tanishito knew it by the second. A man had come in out of the rain and taken the stool at the dead end of the bar, where the light from the stage did not reach. He ordered nothing the first time she came by. He looked at the shelf behind her, then at her hands, then at the door, then at her hands again.

She counted the room. The syndicate men in the booths, their cheap suits dark at the shoulders from the street. Two guns she could see, one she could not. The girls on the floor, working the tables with the practised boredom of people who had been bored on purpose for years. The neon through the front window, pink and then blue, laid across the wet street outside. Pocheasang did this at night, turned the rain into a colour.

The man did not belong to any of it. He did not belong to anything. She looked for what he wanted and found nothing she could give him a face for.

On her third pass he said, “What do you pour for people who don’t drink?”

“Water.”

“Then that.”

She poured it. She gave him the face she kept for quiet men in bad places, a little tired, a little wary, nothing to sell. He looked at it the way you look at a coat on a stranger.

“I used to work the door here,” he said.

“Yeah?”

“Two years.” He did not look at the door. “Back room’s the third one on the left. The owner keeps a ledger in the safe and a bat beside it. The floor in the hall slopes toward the drain, so it doesn’t pool when they hose it down.”

She said nothing. She dried a glass she had already dried.

“There’s a lot out back,” he said. “Streetlight on the corner doesn’t work. I was standing under it with a debt and a stolen rifle and no idea where to put myself. A man was waiting. He said he’d read my file.”

He turned his glass a quarter turn on the wood. He did not drink.

“I read yours.”

The glass in her hand stopped.

“You don’t move like a bartender,” he said. “You watch the room before you watch the drinks. Your hands are never where they’ve been told to be.” His voice was flat and low and had no hurry in it. “I’m not asking you to say anything.”

He took a slip of paper from his jacket and laid it beside the glass. It had a number on it and nothing else.

“Pack what you need. I’ll drive.”

He stood. He left a note for the water, too much, as if he could not make himself leave coins. At the door, where the rain was coming in sideways, he waited with his back to her, not looking round. He was not a man who hurried a person.

## II

The car was black and unmarked, and it smelled of nothing. No air freshener, no old coffee, no cigarette. It was the cleanest vehicle she had ever sat in, and she catalogued it by habit: no radio antenna she could see, a dent in the dash where something had been mounted and removed, a rifle case in the back seat, covered. He had put her bag on top of it without being asked.

The wipers went. The rain came on and the city slid past in smears of wet light, the clubs, the shuttered shops, a man asleep in a doorway with an umbrella over his boots.

He drove with both hands on the wheel. He did not speak. He looked at the road, and at the mirrors, and at the road.

She had expected questions. She had a set of answers ready for every one. Instead there was this, the engine and the wipers and the rain on the roof, and it was the most exposed she had been in weeks. A silence with no one in it to read.

So she did the thing she knew.

She turned in the seat. She let her shoulders drop, and her voice. The tired woman from the bar went out of her like air out of a tyre, and what came in behind it was softer and lower and a little unsteady, a voice that had not been spoken to kindly in a long time and was trying not to show how much it mattered. She let her hand come to rest on the console, an inch from his sleeve.

“It’s been a long time since anyone looked out for me,” she said. “It’s nice.” She let a small breath come after it. “Being seen.”

It was good. It was one of her best. It would make a man feel taller. She had watched it work on cooks and corporals and a night officer in a place she did not name, and she waited for the small shift in his shoulders that told her it had.

He reached out with two fingers and turned the radio down.

It had not been on. The knob clicked on nothing. She understood after a moment that it did not matter. The gesture was the whole of the point.

“You don’t have to do that,” he said.

She did not move. “Do what?”

“That voice.” He did not look at her. “The leaning. I worked the door at that place for two years. I know what it looks like when a woman’s trying to make a man feel important so he won’t hurt her.”

The wipers went across. The hand on the console was still there. She could not think how to take it back.

“You don’t have to trade anything,” he said. His voice was flat. It was not unkind. “Not with me. Not with the man I’m taking you to. You’re just supposed to be here.”

She did not say anything. She had nothing ready.

## III

He kept his eyes on the road, and what he thought was this.

*She’s tragic.*

He had seen it in the club in the first ten minutes. A woman pouring drinks for men with guns, doing the tired act so well it had to have been taught. She did not do it to get by. She did it the way a dog does it that has been kicked at the door too many times, to be small before anyone could decide to be big. And now she had turned it on him in a car, the whole soft voice, the hand on the console, and he did not think she had even chosen it.

He knew the type. He had stood in a doorway for two years and watched it come in and out.

*Konish can help with this.* The man was good at it. He did not fix people the way people fixed them in other places. He looked at what had been done to them, and he did not flinch.

*The world is rotten,* he thought, without heat. It was a bare fact, like the weather.

Hanktonio would take it better if she was not scared. Lykor would tell him on the way in, before she was out of the car. Give her the room on the second floor, the one near the stairs. Let her not eat with them if she did not want to. He turned the matter over the way he turned over everything, looking for the cleanest way through, and found it.

He did not ask what she was running from. It was not his business.

He drove.

## IV

She sat with her hands in her lap, and then did not know where to put them.

They would not stay. They went to her knees, and then to the door handle, and then her own wrists. She folded them. She unfolded them. There was a way a person sat in a car beside another person and she had always had it ready, and now she looked for it and found the place where it was kept, and the place was empty.

Her primary tool had failed. It was not a small thing. It was the thing she had done in every room since she was old enough to learn it, and he had set it down as easily as a man puts down a cup.

She listened for the next move and none came. He did not ask her to be anything. He drove, and the rain came down, and she sat there being a woman in a seat.

It was the worst she had been in weeks. She noted that, clinically, as a thing she would need to report.

## V

He parked at the kerb outside her building and left the engine running.

“I’ll wait,” he said.

She went up. Four flights, the stairwell smelling of damp and boiled cabbage. Her door had a lock that a child could have opened, and she opened it, and stood inside.

It was bare. It had been made bare, a furnished room that had never been lived in, one bed, one chair, a kettle, a window onto an air shaft. She had been there six weeks. There was nothing in it that she had put there.

She did the kit first. The radio went into the sink and she broke the board with the heel of her hand. The papers went into the kettle with a match. The forged work history, the discharge, the debts, curled and blackened and went up in a narrow orange flame, and she watched until nothing legible was left. It was the whole of Dessa Varn, and it took two minutes.

The flat thing from the bar was wrapped in its rag, and she held it a long time. Then she put it in the bag with the rest.

She sat at the little table and wrote the report in the short form. Contact made. Moving. No extraction. She read it back. She did not write the car, or the voice, or the radio he had turned down that was not on. They were not the kind of thing that went in a report. She sent it through the one channel left her and took the card out of the phone and bent it until it cracked.

There was a mirror over the sink. She looked in it.

She tried the voice. *It’s been a long time.* It came out the same as it always had, low and a little broken, the right amount of catch. She watched her own mouth make it. It was perfect, and it was hollow. She heard it as he must have heard it, as a thing she did, with no one inside it.

She stopped.

At the bottom of her coat pocket was a dented teaspoon. She had not put it on any list. She took it out, held it, and put it in the bag with the flat thing and a change of clothes.

She turned out the light. She did not look back at the room. There was nothing in it to look at.

## VI

They drove for a long time. The streets widened. The neon thinned and the glass began, tower on tower, the financial district shining in the wet like something that had not been built by anyone. He took the car down a ramp into a garage the size of a hangar. The concrete was clean and the lights were white. At the far end a private elevator stood open, brass and dark wood, with a camera in the ceiling corner.

He did not speak. He pressed a key to a plate, and the doors closed.

She counted floors. She stopped at forty. She stood with her bag in both hands and her shoulders a little hunched, a woman who had been told to come and did not know why. It was the right posture. She watched herself keep it.

The elevator slowed. The doors opened.

The wind took her breath.

It was a roof the size of a parade ground, lit hard from every side. The rain had thinned to a fine cold blow, and above it lay the sky, low and orange with the city. Soldiers stood at intervals around the edge. Black fatigues, black plate carriers, rifles held low across the chest. Twelve. Thirteen. She did not look at them. She looked at the floor, then at the middle distance, then at the wind-flattened puddles, where a civilian looks who has walked into something she cannot name.

They did not look at her either. They saw the man beside her, and every one of them came to attention, a small shift of boots on wet concrete. Nobody spoke. They were furniture, and she was glad of it.

In the centre of the pad stood a helicopter. It was black, and utility-built, and sleek in the way of things made for work. Along its flank was a stark insignia the colour of old blood, and under it a line of numbers on the tail boom.

The rotors began to turn.

She flinched. It was a small flinch, a woman’s, hand coming up to her face against the downwash. She let the wind pull her hair across her eyes and she let it blind her. Behind the strands, in the half-second of the cover, she was counting. Thirteen guards, rifles, side arms, one with a launcher slung across his back. Two roof exits and the lift. The insignia. The six figures on the tail. She laid them away one at a time for the report she had no one to send.

*Terror,* she thought. She put it on, and it fit.

He stood a pace to her left, with his back to the rotor wash, and looked at her. Under the white lights she could see his face properly for the first time. It was not a hard face. It was a face that had been hard for so long that it had stopped trying.

“Last chance to go back down the elevator,” he said.

He had to raise his voice. He did not sound as if he wanted her to take it.

She looked at the elevator. She looked at the helicopter. She shook her head.

She did not know whether the shake was the cover or her. She had no way of telling.

She climbed in.
