## I

There are twelve doors on the second floor of the house, and eleven of them are never closed.

Samsara Lykor noticed this on his first night. The men of LENDERMAIRE slept with their doors open, left lights burning in the hallway, and drifted toward each other without seeming to notice they were doing it. A man would wake at three in the morning and end up in the kitchen. Then another would appear. Then a third. By dawn, half the house would be sitting around the same table, saying nothing, with the look of people who had been afraid of something and been cured of it by company.

His own door was on the third floor, at the end of the hall. It had a lock. He used it.

## II

He had been in the house for four months when Konish Kimbotin finally asked him about it.

They were on the roof, which Lykor had claimed the way a cat claims a windowsill. No one else came up here. From the edge he could see the whole compound, the perimeter road, and the tree line where the ground fell away. He had counted the approaches the first day. Three. He had worked out the fourth by the second week.

"You don't eat with them," Konish said.

"I eat."

"Alone. After they're finished."

"The food is the same either way."

Konish did not smile. He rarely did. He was a broad, quiet man whose voice never rose, and Lykor had come to understand that this was the most frightening thing about him. Lykor had served under officers who shouted. Shouting was an admission of doubt. Konish never doubted anything out loud.

"The others need the table," Konish said.

"I know."

"You don't."

"No," Lykor agreed. "I don't. That's the difference between us. And it's the only reason you can use me."

The wind moved over the roof. Below them, someone laughed, the bright, unguarded laugh of a man who had never had to wonder whether he belonged somewhere. Lykor listened to it the way a man listens to a language he once studied and forgot.

"Does it bother you?" Konish asked. "That I hold them the way I do."

"Why would it bother me?"

"Most men in your position would be disturbed by it."

"Most men in my position don't get asked." Lykor kept his eyes on the tree line. "You built a leash and put it on twelve necks. I'm not wearing one. What I think of the leash is my own business."

"And yet you stay."

This was the question underneath all the others. Lykor had been waiting for it, and he found he still had no good answer. He turned it over the way he turned over every problem, looking for the angle that would make it simple.

## III

There was no simple answer. The ones he could give were all true, and none of them were enough.

He could say that he had nowhere else to go. That was false. Three weeks ago a man in a pressed suit had sat across from him in a rented room and slid a folder across the table. Inside was an offer from the Ironish Task Force: clean record, back pay, a contractor's salary that would have erased his debts in a year. They had read his Lurkish file. They knew about the raids and the compounds and the men he had walked out of places no one should have walked out of. They did not seem to care about the diagnosis, or the demotion, or the equipment that had gone missing from an armory in the north.

*Everything forgiven,* the folder had said, in the language of contracts. *Come home.*

He could say that he stayed out of ambition. That was false too. LENDERMAIRE would never put his name on a plaque. He was the concealed member. The public did not know he existed, and the men who knew rarely spoke of him. The best thing he could do for the unit was to leave no trace of having been there.

He could say that he stayed because he hated what the Task Force represented, the same machine that had weighed him and found him lacking. But that was only partly true, and he had no use for partial truths.

The real answer was one he did not like to look at directly.

## IV

Years ago, on the night he left the strip club for the last time, a man had been waiting for him in the parking lot. Lykor had a hand on his sidearm before he registered that the man was unarmed. A Cladish man. Broad, still, patient, standing under a broken streetlight as though he had all the time in the world.

Lykor had spent his entire adult life killing men who spoke the way this man spoke. He remembered thinking, with cold clarity, that he could end this in two seconds and walk away.

"I read your file," the man had said. "The real one. Not the one they showed the press."

"Then you know what I am."

"I know what they called you. Individualist. Narcissist." He said the words without weight, the way a man reads a list of ingredients. "I'm not sure they understood what they were looking at."

"And you do."

"No," the man said. "But I'd like to find out. And I'm not going to ask you to become anything else. I want you exactly as you are."

No one had ever said that to him. Not the squad leader who had named him Northrad. Not the therapist with the clipboard. Not his father. Every one of them had wanted something from him, and every one of them had wanted him to be a slightly different shape than he was. The squad leader had wanted a destiny. The military had wanted a team player. Even Lykor had spent years wanting himself to be someone easier to live with.

The Cladish warlord had asked for nothing except what was already there.

Lykor hated the Cladish. He hated them with the settled, uncomplicated hatred of a man who had buried friends because of them. He hated Konish too, in the abstract, as a member of that people, and he suspected he always would.

But hatred and loyalty, he had learned, were not opposites. They could live in the same man, in separate rooms, without ever meeting.

## V

"Samsara," Konish said now, on the roof. It was the first time he had used the first name. "I asked you a question."

Lykor was quiet for a long moment.

"The Task Force offered me a way out," he said at last.

"I know."

"Of course you do." He almost smiled. "I didn't take it."

"I know that too."

"Do you know why?"

"No," said Konish, and Lykor believed him. It was the one thing the man had never pretended to understand. "I've wondered."

"So have I." Lykor finally turned from the tree line. "I've gone through it a hundred times. I don't need the group. I don't need you the way they do. If you vanished tomorrow I'd be fine. I'd find another contract, another war. That's what I am."

"Then why?"

"I don't know." It was strange to say. In all his years, he had never had to. "You looked at what everyone else was afraid of and you didn't flinch. That counts for something, even from a man I should hate. I'm here because I decided to be. Not because you made me. That's all I've got, and it's the only thing about this house that's mine."

Konish studied him. For a moment something moved in the warlord's face, too quick to name. Then it was gone, and he was only a large, patient man standing on a roof in the wind.

"That," Konish said, "is more than any of them can say."

He left without another word. Lykor listened to his footsteps descend the stairs, past the third floor, past the open doors of the second, down to the kitchen where the others were gathering for the evening.

Lykor stayed where he was.

## VI

The sun went down over the tree line. One by one, the lights came on in the windows below, warm yellow squares full of men who could not be alone. He watched them the way he watched everything: from a distance, with a clear field of fire, counting the exits.

Somewhere inside, someone was laughing again.

He did not go down.

But he did not leave, either. And when the night grew cold and his hands began to stiffen on the roof's edge, he understood, with the same quiet certainty he brought to a long shot, that he would be here in the morning. He would be here the morning after that, and the one after that, until the day someone put him in the ground.

Twelve men stayed because they could not do otherwise.

Samsara Lykor stayed because he could.

It was the only kind of loyalty he had ever trusted. It was also, he suspected, the only kind that Konish Kimbotin could never truly command.

Below him, the house glowed against the dark. Above him, the first stars came out, indifferent and cold.

He kept watch.
